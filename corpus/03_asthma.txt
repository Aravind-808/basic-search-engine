TITLE: Asthma - Pathophysiology and Treatment Guidelines

OVERVIEW
Asthma is a chronic inflammatory airway disease characterized by variable and
recurring symptoms of airflow obstruction, bronchial hyperresponsiveness, and
underlying inflammation.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
Chronic inflammation leads to airway hyperresponsiveness, bronchoconstriction,
mucus hypersecretion, and airway remodeling over time. Triggers include
allergens, respiratory infections, exercise, cold air, and irritants such as
smoke.

SYMPTOMS
Characteristic symptoms include wheezing, shortness of breath, chest
tightness, and cough, often worse at night or early morning. Symptoms are
typically episodic and reversible, either spontaneously or with treatment.

CLASSIFICATION
Severity is classified as intermittent, mild persistent, moderate persistent,
or severe persistent based on symptom frequency, nighttime awakenings, and
lung function measurements including FEV1.

DIAGNOSIS
Diagnosis relies on history, physical examination, and spirometry
demonstrating reversible airflow obstruction, typically a >12 percent
improvement in FEV1 after bronchodilator administration. Peak flow monitoring
can assist with ongoing assessment.

TREATMENT
Stepwise management follows a controller/reliever approach. Short-acting
beta-agonists (SABA) such as albuterol provide rapid symptom relief.
Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) form the cornerstone of long-term control,
often combined with long-acting beta-agonists (LABA) for moderate to severe
disease. Leukotriene receptor antagonists, biologics (e.g., omalizumab,
mepolizumab), and oral corticosteroids are reserved for more severe or
refractory cases.

ASTHMA ACTION PLANS
Personalized action plans help patients recognize worsening control and
adjust therapy or seek care based on symptom zones (green, yellow, red).

COMPLICATIONS
Poorly controlled asthma can lead to status asthmaticus, a medical
emergency requiring urgent bronchodilator therapy, systemic corticosteroids,
and possible respiratory support.
