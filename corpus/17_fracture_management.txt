TITLE: Orthopedic Fracture Management

OVERVIEW
Fractures represent a break in bone continuity resulting from trauma,
repetitive stress, or pathological weakening, requiring assessment for
type, stability, and associated soft tissue injury.

CLASSIFICATION
Fractures are classified by pattern (transverse, oblique, spiral,
comminuted), by whether the skin is intact (closed) or breached (open), and
by displacement. Open fractures carry significant infection risk and
require urgent management.

CLINICAL ASSESSMENT
Assessment includes evaluation of pain, swelling, deformity, and
neurovascular status distal to the injury. Compartment syndrome, a surgical
emergency, must be considered in high-energy injuries presenting with
disproportionate pain, pallor, paresthesia, and pulselessness.

IMAGING
Plain radiographs in at least two views are standard for initial fracture
evaluation. CT imaging may be used for complex fractures involving joints,
and MRI can assess associated soft tissue or occult fractures.

INITIAL MANAGEMENT
Initial management includes immobilization, analgesia, and reduction of
displaced fractures when necessary. Open fractures require irrigation,
debridement, and antibiotic prophylaxis to reduce infection risk.

DEFINITIVE TREATMENT
Treatment options range from closed reduction and casting for stable
fractures to open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) with plates,
screws, or intramedullary nails for unstable or displaced fractures.
External fixation may be used for severe open fractures or as a temporizing
measure.

HEALING PROCESS
Fracture healing progresses through hematoma formation, soft callus
formation, hard callus formation, and remodeling, typically requiring
6-12 weeks depending on bone and patient factors.

COMPLICATIONS
Potential complications include malunion, nonunion, infection, compartment
syndrome, and post-traumatic arthritis, underscoring the importance of
appropriate follow-up and rehabilitation.
