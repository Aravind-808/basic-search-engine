TITLE: Thyroid Disorders - Hypothyroidism and Hyperthyroidism

OVERVIEW
The thyroid gland regulates metabolism through production of thyroid
hormones, and dysfunction can manifest as either underactivity
(hypothyroidism) or overactivity (hyperthyroidism).

HYPOTHYROIDISM
Hypothyroidism most commonly results from autoimmune thyroiditis (Hashimoto
thyroiditis) in iodine-sufficient regions, characterized by lymphocytic
infiltration and gradual destruction of thyroid tissue. Symptoms include
fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, hair loss,
and bradycardia.

HYPOTHYROIDISM DIAGNOSIS AND TREATMENT
Diagnosis is confirmed by elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) with
low free thyroxine (T4). Treatment consists of levothyroxine replacement
therapy, titrated based on TSH levels, typically rechecked every 6-8 weeks
after dose adjustments.

HYPERTHYROIDISM
Hyperthyroidism is most commonly caused by Graves disease, an autoimmune
condition involving thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins that activate the
TSH receptor. Other causes include toxic multinodular goiter and toxic
adenoma. Symptoms include weight loss, heat intolerance, palpitations,
tremor, anxiety, and in Graves disease, ophthalmopathy.

HYPERTHYROIDISM DIAGNOSIS AND TREATMENT
Diagnosis is confirmed by suppressed TSH with elevated free T4 and/or T3.
Treatment options include antithyroid medications such as methimazole,
radioactive iodine ablation, and thyroidectomy, chosen based on patient
preference, goiter size, and presence of ophthalmopathy.

THYROID NODULES
Thyroid nodules are common and typically evaluated with ultrasound and, when
indicated, fine-needle aspiration biopsy to assess malignancy risk based on
sonographic features and nodule size.

THYROID FUNCTION TESTING
TSH is generally the most sensitive initial screening test for thyroid
dysfunction, with free T4 and T3 used to further characterize the type and
severity of dysfunction.

PREGNANCY CONSIDERATIONS
Thyroid hormone requirements increase during pregnancy, and untreated
thyroid dysfunction can affect maternal and fetal outcomes, requiring close
monitoring and dose adjustments.
