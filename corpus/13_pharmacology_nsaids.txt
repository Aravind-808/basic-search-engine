TITLE: Pharmacology of NSAIDs

OVERVIEW
Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are among the most widely used
medications for pain, inflammation, and fever, acting through inhibition of
cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes.

MECHANISM OF ACTION
NSAIDs inhibit COX-1 and COX-2 enzymes, reducing the synthesis of
prostaglandins and thromboxanes involved in inflammation, pain signaling,
and platelet aggregation. COX-1 is constitutively expressed and involved in
gastric mucosal protection and platelet function, while COX-2 is induced
during inflammation.

COMMON AGENTS
Widely used NSAIDs include ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, indomethacin,
and aspirin, which irreversibly inhibits COX enzymes. Selective COX-2
inhibitors such as celecoxib were developed to reduce gastrointestinal side
effects.

INDICATIONS
NSAIDs are used for management of osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis,
acute musculoskeletal injuries, dysmenorrhea, headache, and postoperative
pain, as well as antiplatelet effects for cardiovascular prophylaxis with
low-dose aspirin.

ADVERSE EFFECTS
Common adverse effects include gastrointestinal irritation, peptic ulcer
disease, and gastrointestinal bleeding due to reduced mucosal protection.
Renal effects include reduced glomerular filtration and risk of acute kidney
injury, particularly in volume-depleted patients. Cardiovascular risks
include increased risk of myocardial infarction and stroke with certain
agents, particularly at higher doses.

CONTRAINDICATIONS
NSAIDs should be used cautiously or avoided in patients with active
gastrointestinal bleeding, severe renal impairment, heart failure, and in
late pregnancy due to risk of premature closure of the ductus arteriosus.

DRUG INTERACTIONS
NSAIDs can interact with anticoagulants, increasing bleeding risk, and may
reduce the antihypertensive effect of ACE inhibitors and diuretics.

MONITORING
Renal function and gastrointestinal symptoms should be monitored with
long-term NSAID use, particularly in elderly patients.
